Your IDS knows the rules. Driftnet learns your network.

Driftnet is a second layer behind Suricata, Zeek or Snort. It builds a baseline from a recording of your own traffic, then reports the flows and hosts that are rare by that standard, and tells you how rare and why.

Get early access See where it stands

Self-hosted. Scoring is plain numpy: no TensorFlow, no ONNX runtime, no GPU. The daemon makes no outbound connections unless you configure an alert webhook.

How it decides

Normal is whatever your network does.

There is no vendor model and no lab dataset. You record your own traffic, Driftnet fits a baseline to it, and every alert is a statement about that baseline.

01

Same features for learning and scoring

The baseline is built from the same flow records the daemon scores, so a feature never means one thing in training and another live.

02

Each flow against its own kind

A ping is scored against other pings and an HTTPS flow against other HTTPS flows. Being a minority protocol is not an anomaly.

03

Two layers

The flow layer looks at the shape of one connection. The host layer looks at what one host did in a minute, which is where scans, floods and sweeps show up.

04

The threshold is an alert budget

Every score becomes “1 in N flows like the baseline would look this unusual”. You say how many alerts a day you want; the threshold follows.

05

Incidents, not flows

Repeats of the same incident are counted, not re-sent. A one-hour transfer is one alert, not sixty.

06

New services

The first use of a well-known port that never appeared in the baseline is reported: first SSH, first SMB, first telnet.

Under the hood, per context: a rank-Gaussian transform of each feature, a shrinkage Mahalanobis distance, and a small autoencoder's reconstruction error, calibrated on held-out baseline traffic with an extreme-value tail.

Where it sits

Behind the IDS you already run.

Driftnet does not replace Snort, Suricata or Zeek. The signature IDS stays the first layer and owns everything its rules catch. Driftnet learns from the traffic the rules let through and reports what is unusual in that remainder.

01 · your network

Traffic

A live interface, or the logs your sensors already write.

02 · first layer, unchanged

Suricata / Snort / Zeek

Signatures and protocol logging, exactly as you run them today.

03 · second layer

Driftnet

Scores what the rules did not flag. A connection the IDS alerted on is left alone: not scored, not learned as normal.

04 · output

Explained alerts

JSON lines with Suricata-style field names, a text log, or a webhook. For a person to triage. Driftnet only reports; it never blocks.

Coverage

What it catches, and what it does not.

Eleven synthetic attacks were mixed into a day of normal traffic the baseline had never seen, one at a time. Something that is rare is not necessarily malicious, and the reverse.

Caught: 8

Volume, fan-out and never-seen services.

  • 600 MB upload over HTTPS
  • 20 MB upload over HTTPS
  • Port scan, 1,000 ports in 30 seconds
  • Slow scan, 60 ports a minute
  • SMB sweep of a /24
  • SYN flood, 20,000 a minute
  • Telnet scan, 3,000 hosts a minute
  • SSH brute force, 200 tries
Missed: 3

Low-and-slow traffic and rare protocols.

  • DNS tunnel and ICMP tunnel. The small baseline had too little non-web traffic for those contexts to alert at all.
  • Command-and-control beacon, one small request a minute. Traffic that looks like ordinary browsing is out of reach for flow statistics. Catching beacons needs timing analysis across flows, which Driftnet does not do.

Background

Started as a research idea.

Score the flows a signature engine leaves unflagged. The idea was first written up in 2025; the detector has since been rebuilt around per-network baselines.

PAPER · IEEE HiPC 2025 WORKSHOPS

SnortML+: Bridging Rules and Learning for Zero-Day Defense

A two-page paper by Muthuraj Vairamuthu and Harsh Mistry, IIIT-Delhi, proposing an autoencoder that scores the flows Snort did not flag. Driftnet's current detector is a later rewrite and was not evaluated in that paper.
